  しかし、自らが確立した一般相対性理論における重力場方程式の計算上、そのアインシュタインが仮定した＜静的宇宙＞は、天体・物質間に働く万有引力によって形成される強大な重力場へと宇宙そのものが引き寄せられ、収縮し、やがて自らを破壊＿消滅することになる。

  λ、λ、λ、ラムダ…人は死んでも星にはならないし、地獄もなければ天国もない。

  ただ、この広大な宇宙の塵となり、その様々な人々の人生における見果てぬ想いが、目にみえない力、＜ダーク・エネルギー＞となり、宇宙が収縮し潰れぬよう、その内面から宇宙の膨張を促し、加速させ、全消滅へと繋がる万有引力の強大なベクトルの呪縛から、我々を永劫に解き放ち続けるのだ。

λ, λ, λ, lambda… Albert Einstein, in his lifetime, named two things as the ⟨greatest blunder⟩ of his life.

One was signing the letter urging President Roosevelt to build the atomic bomb, in order to resist Nazi Germany.\
The other was the "cosmological term, λ (lambda)."

Holding an unshakable conviction that ⟨the size of the universe must never change, and must remain immutable for all eternity⟩, Einstein assumed the universe to be a "three-dimensional sphere" and built a ⟨static model of the universe⟩, one whose size was eternally constant and isotropic.

But according to the gravitational field equations of the very general theory of relativity he had established, that ⟨static universe⟩ would be drawn by the immense gravitational field formed by universal gravitation between celestial bodies and matter. The universe itself would contract, and in time destroy itself — vanish.

Einstein could never, under any circumstances, admit this contradiction he himself had brought about — the self-destruction of the universe.

To patch this theoretical contradiction that ran against his own belief, he supposed that against "the universal gravitation acting between matter and matter," there must exist an invisible energy supporting the universe from within so that it would not collapse — "a repulsive force acting between space and space." And so Einstein added ⟨a new term⟩, half by force, to the gravitational field equations he himself had completed.\
\
That is the "cosmological term (λgμν) ⇔ cosmological constant (λ), lambda."

![](/%E9%A2%A8%E6%99%AF/%E5%AE%87%E5%AE%99%E5%AE%9A%E6%95%B0.png)

In that moment, Einstein cast aside his standing as a physicist and chose his own arrogance.\
In other words, he told a plain lie.

After the atomic bombs fell on Nagasaki and Hiroshima, Einstein, weeping, apologized to Hideki Yukawa, and then threw himself into the peace movement amid the scorn of other scientists.

But the "cosmological term λ" alone he never admitted, until his death, to be the "greatest blunder of his life" — and it faded from the memory of most people.

Yet many years after Einstein's death, from the standpoint of the quantum theory he so despised, and of the unified theory of ⟨cosmology ⇔ particle physics⟩, the "cosmological term λ" returned as ⟨dark energy⟩, and now it is about to be proven as ⟨observational fact⟩.

λ, λ, λ, lambda… People do not become stars when they die. There is no hell, and no heaven.

We simply become dust of this vast universe, and the unfulfilled longings of all those lives become an invisible force, ⟨dark energy⟩. From within, it urges the universe to expand so that it will not contract and collapse, accelerates it, and keeps freeing us, for all eternity, from the curse of the immense vector of universal gravitation that leads to total annihilation.

Ahead of them, the children will meet with all manner of hardship along the road of life, and will agonize greatly, and struggle and suffer greatly.

Let them agonize.

Let them suffer.

But when the children commit "the greatest blunder of their lives," will we adults be able to stand tall, chests out, and say to them: "There is no such thing as a life that can't be undone"?

λ, λ, λ, lambda… I will hum it over and over, again and again.

That will be our offering of flowers to those who, their longings never fulfilled, turned to dust in this vast.

## A needless addition

I wrote this when I was eighteen, while I was locked up in a juvenile prison. There's something both fresh and nostalgic about the way it tries to demolish logic with a fragile fairy tale. People often tell me, "I can't believe you wrote this at eighteen," but it's exactly the kind of writing only an eighteen-year-old could produce. I certainly couldn't write it now.
